## 1) Towards a Reference Ontology of Money

**Core thesis**

Money is not reducible to the physical or digital substrate that carries it. It is an institutional/status-function phenomenon grounded in normative recognition, collective acceptance, and trust, with validity/value conditions that depend on social-institutional structures rather than brute physical features alone.

**High-value concepts**

- money as status function rather than intrinsic commodity essence
- normative description and institutional recognition behind monetary validity
- nominal value vs exchange value distinction
- monetary object vs currency/value space distinction
- validity phases for money-like objects
- institutional trust as the background condition for monetary operation

**Implications for runtime / glossary**

- Governance Foundation should treat **money** and other monetary-like governance tokens as institutional objects whose status depends on normative frameworks and recognition conditions
- glossary work should distinguish **monetary object**, **currency**, **nominal value**, **exchange value**, **validity**, and **control/holding** semantics
- runtime models should represent not just balances or quantities but the institutional conditions that make a token/object count as money within a system
- monetary governance should preserve transitions in validity, authorization, and acceptable use rather than only raw possession state

**Validation rule candidates**

- a monetary object should be tied to some recognized currency/value system or equivalent normative value space
- monetary validity should be modelable as phase/state semantics rather than assumed permanently true
- exchange/use powers over monetary objects should depend on recognized control/holding and the relevant normative framework
- money-like governance tokens should not be treated as purely brute technical artifacts when institutional status is what makes them operative

## 2) Towards a Legal Core Ontology Based on Alexy’s Theory of Fundamental Rights

**Core thesis**

Legal semantics require a well-founded core ontology that goes beyond flat rule lists. Rights, duties, permissions, and related legal relations should be grounded in triadic/legal-relator structures and normative descriptions, with room for both strict rules and principle-like optimization requirements.

**High-value concepts**

- legal relators as truthmakers for legal relations
- triadic structure for right/duty style relations
- Hohfeldian correlatives such as right-duty and liberty-no-right
- normative descriptions as the institutional basis for legal structures
- rules vs principles distinction
- deontic objects/actions/omissions as the objects of legal relations

**Implications for runtime / glossary**

- Governance Foundation should model legal/governance relations as structured bonds between parties around acts/omissions/objects, not as isolated attributes on a single party
- glossary work should distinguish **right**, **duty**, **permission/liberty**, **claim**, **holder**, **counterparty**, **object of relation**, and **normative description**
- governance models should preserve whether a relation is binary in appearance but triadic in substance (holder, counterparty, object/action)
- principles may need a different treatment from crisp rule constraints when governance semantics are partially satisfied or optimized rather than simply true/false

**Validation rule candidates**

- rights/duties should not exist without explicit holder/counterparty/object semantics
- legal relation objects should identify the relevant act, omission, state, or institutional object they concern
- normative descriptions should ground the existence/recognition of legal core relations where institutional validity matters
- principle-like requirements should not be flattened blindly into simple binary rule semantics when degree/optimization structure matters

## 3) Legal Power-Subjection Relations: Ontological Analysis and Modeling Pattern

**Core thesis**

Legal power is the structured capacity to create, modify, or extinguish legal relations. It is a governance-critical semantic because it explains how institutions change their own normative state through authorized institutional acts.

**High-value concepts**

- power-subjection relator
- institutional acts that alter legal reality
- authorized norm-changing capacity
- original vs derived power
- historical dependence of later legal states on earlier exercises of power
- governance change as mutation of the normative relation graph rather than only event logging

**Implications for runtime / glossary**

- Governance Foundation should treat **power** as the ability to change normative/relational state, not just as influence or social prestige
- runtime state transitions affecting governance rights/duties/permissions should preserve the authority source that made them possible
- glossary work should distinguish **power**, **subjection**, **institutional act**, **authority source**, **derived power**, and **norm-changing event**
- governance history should preserve which legal/governance relations were created, altered, or extinguished by each authorized act

**Validation rule candidates**

- exercises of legal power should point to the normative source that authorizes them
- an institutional act exercising power should have identifiable downstream effect on some legal/governance relation state
- downstream rights/duties/permissions created by power should preserve lineage to the act/power relation that created them
- powers should not be inferred from outcome alone without an explicit authorizing structure when formal governance traceability matters

## 4) Towards an Ontological Analysis of Powertypes

**Core thesis**

Powertypes and multi-level classification matter when governance systems need to represent types whose instances are themselves types, or evolving category schemes whose members and embodiments can change while the higher-order categorization remains identifiable.

**High-value concepts**

- powertype as a type whose instances are themselves types
- variable embodiment / changing members under persistent higher-order identity
- multi-level classification semantics
- distinction between type-level governance category and individual-level membership/instantiation
- regularities attached to higher-order category schemes

**Implications for runtime / glossary**

- Governance Foundation may need powertype-like semantics for category systems such as membership classes, authority classes, entitlement classes, policy categories, or token classes that themselves classify lower-level types
- glossary work should distinguish higher-order category schemes from first-order classes/instances so governance category changes do not collapse into ordinary instance updates
- runtime models should support persistent governance category identity even when the specific embodied member-types or criteria change over time
- multi-level governance taxonomies should remain explicit where “types of types” or rule-schemes-for-rule-schemes are relevant

**Validation rule candidates**

- higher-order category types should identify the lower-level types they govern/classify
- multi-level classification structures should not be flattened into one-level hierarchies where identity semantics are lost
- evolving category schemes should preserve continuity/lineage when their embodied criteria or member-types change
- powertype-like constructs should be introduced explicitly rather than implied accidentally by ambiguous taxonomy usage

## Cross-document synthesis

### Most important convergences

- governance is deeply institutional: money, rights, duties, powers, and category schemes all depend on normative structures rather than brute facts alone
- many governance-relevant relations are not simple attributes but structured relators linking parties, objects/actions, and authorizing descriptions
- institutional change needs explicit modeling of power and norm-changing acts, not only snapshots of current state
- money and other value-bearing governance artifacts only function because of recognized institutional status and authorized control/use semantics
- multi-level categorization matters when governance systems define not just entities, but the types and schemes by which entities are classified and governed

### Runtime model implications

- Governance Foundation should model **institutional facts** distinctly from brute operational facts, while linking the two through events and authorizations
- normative descriptions, legal/governance relators, and power-bearing institutional acts should become first-class runtime semantics rather than hidden assumptions
- monetary/value objects, rights/duties, and powers should all preserve validity, holder/counterparty structure, and lineage to the normative basis that grounds them
- governance engines should be able to explain not just what the current state is, but what authorized act and what normative source produced that state
- multi-level category schemes may be necessary for governance artifacts such as role classes, entitlement classes, policy classes, and membership/tier systems

### Validation rule candidates

1. No legal/governance right-duty relation without explicit holder, counterparty, and object/action structure.
2. No norm-changing act without an authorizing power relation or normative source.
3. No money-like institutional object without recognized value-system and validity semantics.
4. Preserve lineage from institutional act to derived legal/governance state.
5. Distinguish principle-like optimization semantics from strict rule semantics where governance requires it.
6. Introduce multi-level/powertype structures explicitly when governing category schemes rather than flattening them.

## Immediate design consequences for Governance Foundation

The clearest next modeling move from this cluster is to strengthen the ontology around:

- **institutional fact vs brute fact distinctions**
- **legal/governance relators** for rights, duties, permissions, and claims
- **power/subjection semantics** for who can change governance state and under what authority
- **money/value artifacts as normative institutional objects**
- **lineage-preserving governance state transitions** tied to institutional acts and authorizing descriptions
- **multi-level governance category schemes** where types-of-types or evolving classification frameworks matter

This Tier 3 cluster pushes Governance Foundation toward a more formally governable normative core: one that can represent not just structure and service, but the institutional basis for value, obligation, authority, and legitimate change.
